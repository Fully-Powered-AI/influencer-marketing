## 1. FTC Endorsement Guides (2023 revision)

Status and citation
- The FTC announced the updated Endorsement Guides on 29 June 2023 (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/06/federal-trade-commission-announces-updated-advertising-guides-combat-deceptive-reviews-endorsements, read 2026-10-20).
- The revised Guides were published at 88 FR 48092 on 26 July 2023 and are codified at 16 CFR Part 255 (search-result summary of https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/07/26/2023-14795/guides-concerning-the-use-of-endorsements-and-testimonials-in-advertising, read 2026-10-20; the page itself was not opened).
- The Guides "don't have the force of law". Practices inconsistent with them can still lead to enforcement under Section 5 of the FTC Act (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).

What counts as a material connection
- 16 CFR 255.5(a) says a connection the audience would not reasonably expect "must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously". It "can include a business, family, or personal relationship" and "can include monetary payment or the provision of free or discounted products"; benefits "such as early access to a product" also count (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.5, read 2026-10-20).
- Disclosure is needed when "a significant minority of the audience" does not understand or expect the connection (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.5, read 2026-10-20).
- Affiliate commissions: in Example 11 of 255.5(b), a blogger who earns a share of sales through affiliate links "should clearly and conspicuously disclose the compensation" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.5, read 2026-10-20).
- Free product: if a brand gave "anything of value", including free or discounted products, the influencer should disclose it, even when mentioning a different product from the same brand (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, dated November 2019, read 2026-10-20).
- Discount codes: the FTC FAQ says a personalised code may show a relationship but not necessarily a financial one, and recommends "erring on the side of disclosure" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).
- Affiliate-link wording: "Affiliate link" alone and "buy now" buttons are not adequate, and "commissionable link" is "probably not a clear disclosure". "Paid link" next to the link "should be an adequate disclosure", as should a sentence such as "I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).

What "clear and conspicuous" requires
- Definition, summarised from 16 CFR 255.0(f): the disclosure must be difficult to miss and easily understandable by ordinary consumers. It goes in the visual portion, the audible portion, or both, depending on how the claim is made. In interactive media it must be unavoidable, and nothing else in the communication may contradict it (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.0, read 2026-10-20).
- Placement: the disclosure belongs with the endorsement itself. Disclosures on a profile or About page, at the end of a post, behind a "MORE" link, or mixed into a group of hashtags or links are likely to be missed (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, read 2026-10-20).
- Video: put the disclosure in the video itself, not only in the description, and make it both audible and on screen. In live streams, repeat it periodically (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, read 2026-10-20). The FAQ also recommends a continuous disclosure through a live stream (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).
- Stories and images: superimpose the disclosure on the image for long enough to be read (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, read 2026-10-20).
- Platform tools: do not assume a platform's built-in disclosure tool is sufficient; use it in addition to your own disclosure (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, read 2026-10-20). The 2023 press release says a platform tool "might not be an adequate disclosure" (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/06/federal-trade-commission-announces-updated-advertising-guides-combat-deceptive-reviews-endorsements, read 2026-10-20). The FAQ says "the ultimate responsibility... rests with the influencer and the brand – not the platform" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).
- Wording that works: "advertisement", "ad" and "sponsored"; "#ad", "Ad:" and "Paid ad"; "Gifted by XYZ" for free product (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers and https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).
- Wording that fails: "sp", "spon", "collab", and "thanks" or "ambassador" on their own (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, read 2026-10-20). The FAQ calls "Gifted" alone "likely to be ambiguous" and "#ambassador" or "partner" "ambiguous and confusing". It says "#comped" and "#hosted" are not sufficiently clear, and that "thank you" alone is not enough (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).
- Posting from outside the US: US law applies if the post can reasonably be expected to affect US consumers (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, read 2026-10-20).

The advertiser's duty to instruct and monitor
- Advertisers "need to have reasonable programs in place to train and monitor members of their network". The FAQ lists four elements: explain which claims are permitted, instruct on disclosure, periodically search what endorsers say, and "take appropriate action if you find questionable practices" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).
- For ephemeral content, the FAQ suggests requiring "that paid posts aren't made without you approving them in advance" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).
- Using an agency does not shift the duty. The company "is ultimately responsible for what others do on your behalf" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).
- No monitoring percentage or frequency is specified (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides, read 2026-10-20).

Liability for what endorsers say
- 16 CFR 255.1(d): "Advertisers are subject to liability for misleading or unsubstantiated statements made through endorsements", and also for failing to disclose unexpected material connections (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.1, read 2026-10-20).
- 16 CFR 255.1(e): endorsers "may be liable for statements made in the course of their endorsements" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.1, read 2026-10-20).
- 16 CFR 255.1(f): agencies and other intermediaries "may be liable for their roles in creating or disseminating endorsements" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.1, read 2026-10-20).
- Endorsers must not talk about a product they have not tried. They must not call a product they thought was bad great, or make claims that need proof the advertiser does not have, "such as scientific proof that a product treats a health condition" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers, read 2026-10-20).
- Health claims generally need "competent and reliable scientific evidence", usually "randomized, controlled human clinical testing". An advertiser cannot make through a testimonial a claim it could not make directly (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance, read 2026-10-20; the issue date is not shown on the page).

## 2. Civil penalties and related rules

- Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule (16 CFR Part 465). The FTC announced the final rule on 14 August 2024. It bans fake or false reviews and testimonials (including AI-generated ones), reviews bought conditional on sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled "independent" review sites, review suppression, and buying or selling fake social media indicators such as bot followers. Its purpose is to "allow the agency to seek civil penalties against knowing violators" (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials, read 2026-10-20).
- Effective date of Part 465: 60 days after Federal Register publication, according to the press release. Search-result coverage gives publication as 22 August 2024 and the effective date as 21 October 2024 (https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/08/ftc-issues-final-rule-on-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials, read 2026-10-20; secondary).
- Inferred: Part 465 does not by itself turn every disclosure point in the Endorsement Guides into a penalty rule. It does cover fake engagement and insider testimonials, which are relevant when screening creators.
- Penalty amount: the maximum for Section 5(m)(1)(A) violations rose from USD 51,744 to USD 53,088 in the 2025 adjustment (https://www.ftc.gov/node/87476, FTC press release dated 11 February 2025, read 2026-10-20). A search-result summary says the FTC kept the 2025 levels for 2026 (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-13/pdf/2026-14060.pdf, not opened, read 2026-10-20). Verify.

## 3. Optional restricted-category example: personal lubricants

Classification
- The FDA product-classification database lists device name "lubricant, personal", product code NUC, under regulation 21 CFR 884.5300 ("Condom"). It is Class 2, the submission type is 510(k), the review panel is Obstetrics/Gynecology, and it is eligible for third-party review. The recognized consensus standard listed is ASTM D7661-18 for compatibility of personal lubricants with natural rubber latex condoms (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPCD/classification.cfm?ID=NUC, read 2026-10-20).
- The database definition describes the device as "a personal lubricant, for penile and/or vaginal application". It is intended to moisturize and lubricate, to ease intimate activity, and to supplement natural lubrication. Compatibility with natural rubber latex, polyisoprene and polyurethane condoms is stated per device (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPCD/classification.cfm?ID=NUC, read 2026-10-20).
- The text of 21 CFR 884.5300 itself covers condoms: Class II (special controls) for latex and non-latex condoms. It does not name lubricants (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/884.5300, read 2026-10-20).
- openFDA counts 249 510(k) decisions under product code NUC, from 2000-08-02 to 2026-09-23 (https://api.fda.gov/device/510k.json?search=product_code:NUC&count=decision_date, read 2026-10-20).

Cleared indications are product-specific
- An oil-based example: the indications for use in K171985, Astroglide O Oil Personal Lubricant and Massage Oil (cleared 30 August 2017), read "a personal lubricant for penile and/or vaginal application". The indications also carry a sentence that is font-encoded in the PDF text layer; decoded, it reads "This product is not compatible with condoms" (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf17/K171985.pdf, read 2026-10-20).
- An anal-use example: K230674, JO Water Based Anal Thick Lubricant (cleared 8 May 2023), reads "a personal lubricant for penile, anal and/or vaginal application". It also states compatibility with natural rubber latex and polyisoprene condoms and incompatibility with polyurethane condoms (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf23/K230674.pdf, read 2026-10-20).
- Inferred from these two letters: anal use and each condom-compatibility statement are written into a specific product's cleared indications. A creator claim such as "safe for anal", "latex-safe" or "works with any non-latex condom" is a regulated statement about the device. It should be checked against that product's own 510(k) indications, not written as copy.

Condoms and oil
- In FDA's labeling guidance for natural rubber latex condoms, the recommended labeling reads "DO NOT USE OIL-BASED LUBRICANTS, such as those made with petroleum jelly (e.g., Vaseline), mineral oil, vegetable oil, or cold cream, as these may damage the condom". It also recommends "If you add lubricant, use a water-based lubricant" (https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/guidance-documents-medical-devices-and-radiation-emitting-products/labeling-natural-rubber-latex-condoms-classified-under-21-cfr-8845300-class-ii-special-controls, guidance issued 23 December 2008, content current as of 11/12/2021, read 2026-10-20).
- The same guidance's recommended risk statements: "Condoms reduce the overall risk of STI transmission" and "Latex condoms do not completely eliminate the risks of pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections (STIs)" (same URL, read 2026-10-20).
- Polyisoprene: a WHO lubricant document is reported to say that polyisoprene condoms are as sensitive to oil-based lubricants as latex ones, while polyurethane tolerates most lubricants (search-result summary of a mirror copy at https://www.e-lactancia.org/media/papers/Lubricants-WHO2012.pdf, read 2026-10-20; secondary, not opened).
- Inferred: "not latex-compatible, can be used with non-latex condoms" is broader than the polyurethane-only compatibility that oil-based products typically carry. Treat condom compatibility as a product-safety statement taken verbatim from cleared labeling.
- Inferred: no personal lubricant indication seen in the two letters above mentions STI, HIV or disease prevention. Any such claim by a creator would go beyond a lubricant's cleared indications.

## 4. Optional restricted-category examples: desensitizers and supplements

Historical category examples only: apply these source leads only if the actual programme includes these product types, after verifying the current rules and product evidence. No brand inventory is supplied by this package.

- Benzocaine desensitizers fall under the OTC monograph for male genital desensitizers. 21 CFR 348.10(a) allows "Benzocaine, 3 to 7.5 percent in a water-soluble base" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/348.10, read 2026-10-20).
- 21 CFR 348.50 fixes the indications, for example "Helps in the prevention of premature ejaculation", and the warnings, for example "Premature ejaculation may be due to a condition requiring medical supervision". The benzocaine directions read "Apply a small amount to head and shaft of penis before intercourse, or use as directed by a doctor" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/348.50, read 2026-10-20).
- Inferred: the monograph indications and directions mention the penis only. Anal-numbing claims are outside those indications and need regulatory review before a creator repeats them.
- Supplements: structure/function claims carry a disclaimer that the product is not intended to "diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease", because "only a drug can legally make such a claim". FDA must be notified of a claim within 30 days of marketing (https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/structurefunction-claims, content current as of 03/28/2024, read 2026-10-20).
- FDA warns that many products marketed for sexual enhancement "are likely to be contaminated with dangerous hidden ingredients" and are "sometimes falsely advertised as dietary supplements, food and/or all-natural treatments" (https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/sexual-enhancement-and-energy-product-notifications, content current as of 10/15/2026, read 2026-10-20).
- Inferred: creator copy for the supplement should not compare it to erectile-dysfunction prescriptions or promise treatment results. Counsel should review the supplement claims before any creator brief is written.

## 5. Platform rules, by platform

### Meta (Instagram and Facebook)

- Brand ads, allowed with conditions. Meta's Health and Wellness ad standard lists "Lube and pheromones" among products advertisers may promote "When targeting people 18 years or older", "as long as the focus is on health". Premature-ejaculation treatments and "Pain relief during sex" are on the same permitted list (https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness, last updated 22 July 2026, read 2026-10-20).
- Brand ads, prohibited. "Ads must not promote the sale or use of adult sexual arousal products or services" unless the ad is health-focused and 18+, and pleasure- or enhancement-focused products such as "Sex toys" and "non-surgical genital enhancement products" are prohibited (same URL, read 2026-10-20).
- Supplement ads must be "targeted to people at least 18 years or older" (same URL, read 2026-10-20).
- Creator sponsored content (branded content). Meta's Branded Content Policies list a restriction on "Adult products or services, except for family planning and contraception" (search-result excerpt of https://en-gb.facebook.com/business/help/221149188908254, read 2026-10-20; the page body did not render). Inferred: a lubricant is not contraception, so creator branded content about it may fall under this restriction even though the ads policy allows lube ads. This needs confirming with Meta before the programme relies on Instagram branded content.
- Labelling: branded content must tag the business partner with the paid-partnership tool (unsourced, from training knowledge; verify against the Branded Content Policies page above).
- Age gating: see section 6.

### TikTok

- Brand ads, allowed with conditions. In the United States section of the Healthcare and Pharmaceuticals policy, condoms and lubricants are allowed if advertisers "Restrict the ads to audiences aged 18 years or older" and "Do not focus on sexual pleasure or be overtly sexual in nature" (https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/tiktok-ads-policy-healthcare-pharmaceuticals, last updated September 2026, read 2026-10-20).
- Brand ads, prohibited. The Adult Content ad policy says "Sexual products are not allowed", with examples including "sex toys". It also prohibits promoting "the enhancement of sexual performance, pleasure" (https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/tiktok-ads-policy-adult-content, last updated August 2026, read 2026-10-20). Inferred: a lubricant ad has to read as sexual health, not pleasure, or it falls under this page.
- Creator sponsored content: prohibited. The Branded Content Policy lists adult products "such as pornography, sex toys, lubricants and fetish costumes" as prohibited (https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/bc-policy/en, published 4 August 2026, effective 31 August 2026, read 2026-10-20).
- Labelling: "When posting Branded Content, you must enable the commercial content disclosure toggle" (same URL, read 2026-10-20).
- Age gating: see section 6.

### YouTube (and Google Ads)

- Brand ads. Google Ads allows "personal lubricants" on "the Google Display Network and YouTube in limited cases". The ads may not focus on "sexual pleasure or enhancement", may not use "sexually suggestive elements and themes", and may "only target users 18 years or older". Permitted locations: Australia, Brazil, Canada, Germany, Portugal, New Zealand, Spain, United Kingdom, United States (https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15275138, read 2026-10-20).
- Under the separate Sexual content policy, "Sexual merchandise" ("Sex toys", "Sexual enhancers") is moderately restricted, and such ads cannot run on YouTube (https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6023699, read 2026-10-20).
- Creator sponsored content. Creators declare it by "selecting the paid promotion button" ("Yes, my video includes branded content"), which "adds a disclosure label that appears at the beginning of your video". Branded content must comply with Google Ads policies, "with some exceptions" (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235, read 2026-10-20). Inferred: sponsored lubricant videos are possible if they stay health-focused and non-suggestive under the lubricant policy above.
- Legal duty: creators and brands are responsible for "complying with all applicable legal requirements". Under the FTC guidance in section 1, the YouTube label alone is not sufficient (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/154235, read 2026-10-20).
- Age gating: see section 6.

## 6. Age gating

Optional restricted-category example only. The following historical observations concern adult sexual-wellness products; they are not default audience restrictions for this package:
- Meta lube ads must target 18+ (https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness, read 2026-10-20).
- TikTok US condom and lubricant ads must be restricted to 18+ (https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/tiktok-ads-policy-healthcare-pharmaceuticals, read 2026-10-20).
- Google lubricant ads must target 18+ (https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15275138, read 2026-10-20).
- Facebook Pages Terms, section IV.A: Pages promoting the private sale of regulated goods "(including firearms, alcohol, tobacco, or adult products)" "must restrict access to a minimum age of 18" (https://www.facebook.com/policies/pages_groups_events/, last revised 8 March 2018, read 2026-10-20).

Tools a creator can use on a post or account:
- Instagram, per post. Creators with access to the paid-partnership label can set a minimum age and location restriction on feed posts, Reels, videos and stories. The restriction cannot be added or changed after publishing (search-result excerpt of https://help.instagram.com/316932422966736, read 2026-10-20).
- Instagram, per account. Professional accounts can set a default minimum age, or ages by country, under the tools for business or creator accounts (search-result excerpt of https://help.instagram.com/iphone-app/853772598370828, read 2026-10-20).
- Facebook Pages: Settings and privacy, then Followers and public content, then Age restrictions; this needs full control of the Page (search-result excerpt of https://www.facebook.com/help/www/778445532225441, read 2026-10-20).
- YouTube: in YouTube Studio, Audience, then "Age restriction (advanced)", select "Yes, restrict my videos to viewers over 18". Viewers must then be signed in and 18 or over. YouTube warns that videos intended as ads should not use this, because the ads will be permanently disapproved (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2950063, read 2026-10-20). Age-restricted videos "may have limited or no ads monetization" (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2802167, read 2026-10-20).
- TikTok: creators can tag a video or LIVE as suitable only for viewers 18 and over through Audience Controls; tagged content is hidden from under-18 users and is still moderated (search-result excerpts of https://support.tiktok.com/en/safety-hc/account-and-user-safety/audience-controls and https://www.tiktok.com/safety/en/tools-and-guides/live-safety-guide, read 2026-10-20; the support page did not render).
- X: mark media as sensitive. Under-18 users and users without a birth date cannot view it (https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/adult-content, search-result excerpt, read 2026-10-20).
- Grindr: the app is adults-only, "at least 18, or older in certain jurisdictions" (https://www.grindr.com/community-guidelines, read 2026-10-20). Scruff: minimum age not found on the pages read (https://support.scruff.com/portal/kb/articles/profile-guidelines and https://advertise.perrystreet.com/, read 2026-10-20).

What a brand can require:
- Inferred: no platform found lets the brand switch on age restriction for a creator's organic post. The brand can require it in the creator agreement. On Meta, Google and TikTok the brand controls 18+ targeting on any paid amplification it runs itself.
- Inferred: under the FTC duty to instruct and monitor (section 1), checking that the age setting was applied fits the same review step as checking the disclosure.

## 7. UK and EU (secondary)

Secondary, relevant only if the brand sells to the UK or EU. The ASA's "Influencers' guide to making clear that ads are ads" (third edition, 23 March 2023) is the UK reference. The guide says the advice "remains broadly correct" but that its legal underpinning will change under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (https://www.asa.org.uk/resource/influencers-guide.html, read 2026-10-20). The ASA expects an upfront label such as "Ad", "Advert", "Advertising", "Advertisement" or "Advertisement Feature", with or without "#". It advises against "Gifted", "sp", "spon", "aff" and "affiliate", and says brand tags, campaign hashtags and offer codes alone are not enough (search-result excerpts of https://www.asa.org.uk/news/ad-vice-making-clear-that-an-ad-is-an-ad.html and https://www.asa.org.uk/static/9cc1fb3f-1288-405d-af3468ff18277299/INFLUENCERGuidanceupdatev6HR.pdf, read 2026-10-20). In the EU, the Commission's Influencer Legal Hub applies the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. Disclosure is triggered by any consideration, including affiliate percentages and free products, and must be prominent from the outset. Hashtags at the end of a long caption or behind "read more" are inadequate, and each post needs its own label (search-result excerpt of https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/74f60707-1207-4a6f-a9aa-033ca8a3d382_en?filename=Legal+brief+6.pdf, read 2026-10-20).

## 8. Caption preview lengths

How many characters of a caption or description show before the "more" fold sets how early a disclosure must appear to be seen without a tap. The FTC's guidance treats a disclosure behind a "MORE" link as likely to be missed (section 1), and the EU guidance treats hashtags behind "read more" as inadequate (section 7). Where a platform states the figure, it is quoted below. Where it does not, the line says "not stated by the platform" and gives the commonly cited figure, labelled "secondary source". Secondary figures are approximate: device, screen width, line breaks and app version all move the fold. Every source was read on 2026-10-20.

### Instagram

- Fold: not stated by the platform. Instagram's help page on adding a caption gives no length or truncation figure (https://help.instagram.com/236245819849257, read 2026-10-20). Meta's Instagram Platform reference gives only the cap for captions published through the API: "Maximum 2200 characters, 30 hashtags, and 20 @ tags." (https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-user/media, read 2026-10-20).
- Secondary source: "only the first ~125 characters appear in feed before Instagram cuts the rest with a "...more" link", and "The same limits apply to Reels, carousels, and standard photo posts." The same page says "Instagram doesn't publish an official "see more" threshold" (https://www.hootsuite.com/social-media-tools/social-media-caption-preview, read 2026-10-20).

### TikTok

- Organic fold: not stated by the platform. TikTok's Content Posting API gives only the cap for captions posted through it: "The maximum length is 2200 in UTF-16 runes." (https://developers.tiktok.com/doc/content-posting-api-reference-direct-post, read 2026-10-20). The in-app caption cap was not found on a TikTok page; searched tiktok.com, support.tiktok.com and newsroom.tiktok.com for "4,000 characters" and caption limits (search results, read 2026-10-20).
- Ad captions, stated by TikTok: "Max 4 lines can be displayed. If exceeding 4 lines, the text would be covered by a See more message." TikTok suggests keeping ad captions "within 50 characters for CN/JP/KR or 100 characters in other languages to avoid being covered by" See more, and says "Depending on the phone model and operating system, the maximum number of characters that can be displayed in one single line differs." For Spark Ads, "Ad captions for Spark Ads are extracted directly from the organic video captions" (https://ads.tiktok.com/resources/help/article/tiktok-reservation-in-feed-ads-reach-frequency?lang=en, last updated July 2025, read 2026-10-20).
- Secondary source, for organic posts: "TikTok now allows captions up to 4,000 characters, but the in-feed preview cuts around 100 characters before the "...more" expansion." (https://www.hootsuite.com/social-media-tools/social-media-caption-preview, read 2026-10-20).
- Inferred: a Spark Ad reuses the creator's organic caption, so a disclosure inside the first 100 characters shows on the organic post and on any paid amplification of it.

### YouTube (video descriptions and Shorts)

- Watch page, stated in lines, not characters: "You can think about the description as two parts - what viewers see before clicking “Show more” on desktop or “more” on mobile and what they see after. Consider using the first few lines of your description to describe your video because it’s what viewers will see first." The cap: "The description allows a maximum of 5000 characters." (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12948449?hl=en, read 2026-10-20).
- Watch-page fold in characters: not stated by the platform (same URL, read 2026-10-20). Secondary source: "only the first ~100 characters appear above the "...more" expansion under the video on desktop. Mobile shows slightly less." (https://www.hootsuite.com/social-media-tools/social-media-caption-preview, read 2026-10-20).
- Shorts: not stated by the platform. YouTube's help page on watching Shorts does not say where or how much of the description shows in the Shorts player (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12319243?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DAndroid, read 2026-10-20). Secondary source, a tool vendor's blog dated 23 May 2026: "only the first 100 characters appear above the fold on the Shorts player. Everything else is hidden behind the description expand button." It also says "YouTube already shows the title above the description." (https://hashtagtools.io/blog/youtube-shorts-character-limits-title-description-hashtags-2026, read 2026-10-20).
- Inferred: on Shorts the title and the video itself are what a viewer sees first, so the spoken and on-screen disclosure that the FTC asks for in the video (section 1) carries the weight, and the description disclosure backs it up.

### X

- Fold: not stated on any X page we could read. X's help pages returned HTTP 403 to our fetcher (https://help.x.com/en/using-x/x-premium-how-to, read 2026-10-20). A search-result summary of that page gives 25,000 characters for longer posts by Premium subscribers (search results, read 2026-10-20). X's developer data dictionary defines `note_tweet` as containing "the full text of a Post for long-form Posts (>280 characters)" (https://docs.x.com/x-api/fundamentals/data-dictionary, read 2026-10-20).
- Secondary source, standard posts: "The 280-character cap is the post itself: there's no "see more" because the entire post fits in feed." (https://www.hootsuite.com/social-media-tools/social-media-caption-preview, read 2026-10-20).
- Secondary source, longer posts: "You will see only the first 280 characters on the timeline", with "Show more" to read the rest (https://techcrunch.com/2023/02/08/twitter-blue-introduces-4000-character-tweets-says-half-ads-coming-soon, 8 February 2023, read 2026-10-20; press coverage of the launch, when the cap was 4,000).
- Inferred: on X, a disclosure in the first 280 characters is visible without a tap.

### Facebook

- Fold: not stated by the platform. No Facebook Help Center page on post truncation was found; searched facebook.com, meta.com and transparency.meta.com for "See more" and long-post truncation (search results, read 2026-10-20). Meta's Ads Guide gives a recommended length for ad text, "Primary Text: 50-150 characters", with no truncation figure (https://www.facebook.com/business/ads-guide/update/image/facebook-feed/link-clicks, read 2026-10-20).
- Secondary source, inconsistent within itself: its cheat sheet says "Feed truncates around 280 characters with a "See more" link", while its method note says "Reported cuts range from ~140 characters (mobile, narrow viewports) to ~300 characters (desktop, wider viewports)" and that it uses 200 "as a conservative middle" (https://www.hootsuite.com/social-media-tools/social-media-caption-preview, read 2026-10-20).
- Inferred: write for the low end, about 140 characters, so a disclosure shows on narrow phones.

### What this means for disclosure placement

- Inferred: the shortest folds cited are about 100 characters (TikTok, YouTube descriptions and Shorts), then about 125 (Instagram) and about 140 (Facebook on mobile). A disclosure inside the first 100 characters, best at the very start, is visible without a tap on every platform above. Line breaks, emoji and screen width move the fold, so the review step should check the posted caption on a phone.
- Inferred: on video platforms the caption is not the main disclosure. The FTC asks for the disclosure in the video itself (section 1); the caption disclosure is in addition to it.
