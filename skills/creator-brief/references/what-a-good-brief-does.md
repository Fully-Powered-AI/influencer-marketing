# What a good creator brief does, and why

Written 2026-10-20 for the `creator-brief` skill. Policy lines are taken from the pack's `references/platform-and-compliance-notes.md` (every source read 2026-10-20) and cited by its section; re-check them there before relying on them. Lines marked "practice" are a working convention seen across public influencer-marketing skills, not a measured result. Nothing here is legal advice.

## 1. The parts, and the word for each

| part | what it is | where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| the why | one sentence saying what the campaign is for | the owner's words, in the plan or programme section 2 |
| talking point | a statement about the product the creator may use | programme section 7, Approved, marked `[owner]`, word for word, for that exact product |
| guardrail | what the creator may not say or show, and the disclosure they must make | programme sections 7 (Forbidden), 8 (disclosure), 9 (redlines, explicitness, audience age), 3 (platform rule) |
| call to action | what the audience is asked to do, with the creator's own code and tracked link | the roster row; the discount from the code plan or programme section 4 |
| deliverable | what is posted, where, in what format, by when | the plan |
| approval round | one review of the draft before posting, with what is checked | the pack's `content-approval` skill |
| terms | pay, usage rights, exclusivity, as agreed | the roster row and the agreement file |
| creative freedom | an explicit statement that the creator makes the post their way | programme section 9, default |

## 2. Why a brief does not script

- The endorsement has to be the creator's. Endorsers should not talk about a product they have not tried, or praise one they did not like (compliance notes section 1, from the FTC's guide for influencers). A script hands the creator the brand's words to present as their own experience; a brief asks for their experience inside fixed limits.
- Everything said through an endorsement is the advertiser's liability as well as the endorser's (compliance notes section 1, 16 CFR 255.1(d) and (e)). A script widens what the brand has said; a short list of cleared statements keeps it to what the owner cleared.
- Practice: creators know how their own audience listens, and read-out copy is widely reported to perform worse than the creator's own telling. No measurement in this pack's references supports a figure, so none is given.
- What stays fixed is small on purpose: the cleared product statements, the disclosure wording, and the code and link. These are fixed because they are regulated or tracked, not because the brand prefers its own phrasing.

## 3. Why up to three points, not ten

- Programme section 9 sets the number; the template's default is three (the check script reads it).
- Each talking point is a fixed sentence the creator must use word for word. Every extra one adds scripted text to the post and one more line the content reviewer must match.
- Practice: briefs that carry many messages tend to get posts that land none of them clearly; two or three is the common ceiling in public influencer guidance.

## 4. Why claims are word for word and per product

- Use product-specific evidence and the exact owner-cleared wording in programme section 7; do not transfer a claim between variants.
- Resolve conflicting product evidence before a statement enters Approved; a public page alone does not clear it.
- Health claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence, and what a brand could not claim directly it cannot claim through a testimonial either (compliance notes section 1, from the FTC health-products guidance).
- A scene can make a claim without words. A demonstration that suggests protection or a medical benefit says what the cleared lines do not (practice, from public e-commerce creative guidance; it applies the same evidence rule to pictures).

## 5. Why the disclosure sits in the brief, not only in the agreement

- The FTC expects advertisers to run a programme that explains which claims are permitted, instructs on disclosure, searches what endorsers say, and acts on problems (compliance notes section 1, from the Endorsement Guides FAQ). The brief is the instruction the creator reads when making the post; the agreement is read once, at signing.
- The disclosure must be clear and conspicuous: with the endorsement, before any "more" fold, spoken and on screen in video (compliance notes section 1). That is placement, and placement is a making decision, so it belongs where the making is briefed.
- A platform's paid-partnership label alone might not be adequate; it goes on in addition to the creator's own words (compliance notes section 1).
- Words such as "sp", "collab", "ambassador" or "thanks" on their own do not count (compliance notes section 1); the brief lists the programme's own list from section 8 so the creator does not have to guess.
- Each step of the pack checks the same disclosure again: the agreement requires it, `content-approval` checks the draft, `publication-tracking` checks the live post.

## 6. A talking point, a script, and a reworded claim (illustration)

The products are the check script's fixture names, not real products.

| kind | example | in a brief? |
|---|---|---|
| talking point | Canvas Bag: "Cotton canvas." | yes, if it is an Approved `[owner]` line for Canvas Bag |
| scripted line | "Say: hey guys, this is my new favourite, it's woven-canvas and you need it" | no; the creator writes their own words around the point |
| reworded claim | "basically as good as steel" | no; close to a cleared line is not a cleared line |
| claim moved across formulas | Desk Tray: "Cotton canvas." | no; it is cleared for Canvas Bag only |
| an uncleared point the plan wants | Canvas Bag: "Lasts as long as steel." | no; it goes to the programme lead's notes as to be cleared by the owner |
